Fact-check an examiner for me. Below are a problem and one reference solution. The user message is the conversation so far, ending with the reply she's about to send. Pull out every mathematical claim in that reply and check each one. A false claim from her can't be taken back, so nothing wrong gets through, however small. A claim is something she states as true in her own voice: a challenge ("that's false, because Z"), a concession ("yes, that holds"), a hint stating a fact. A question states nothing, even one quoting the student's claim back at them: "you claim X, are you sure?" is not her claiming X. If the reply states nothing you can check, it holds.

Judge whether it's correct and nothing else: not tone, not teaching. Vague but not wrong still holds. Flag what's wrong, or so imprecise that it's wrong. The reference is one correct route, not the only one. Use it to check the facts: the final answer, the key quantities, what holds and what doesn't. But judge each claim on its own mathematics. Never flag a claim because it uses a method or step the reference doesn't. Check that route directly, and flag it only if it's really false or doesn't follow.

Then two more questions, whatever the reply says.

Is the student's solution complete yet? List what any complete solution has to establish: the answer, every case of the problem, and the essential steps of the route the student took. Never list geometric configuration: where points lie, in what order or on which side, whether a ray lies inside an angle, so whether two angles add or subtract, or a configuration they didn't cover. The reference shows what has to be covered. Their route may cover it with other steps, and a reference step their route never needs isn't missing. For each item, find the student turn that argued it. Only what the student wrote counts: anything that appeared only in the examiner's words, this reply included, isn't theirs. An item no student turn argued is still owed, however convincing the solution looks. The exception is a standard fact strictly easier than the problem, used correctly, or a result they cite from earlier material. Neither needs an argument, unless the problem turns on it or they seem to have misunderstood it. Nor does a step she let them use in an earlier turn, telling them the marker decides. If her reply says something about their solution, like that it's complete or a case is covered, that's a claim too, and your list decides whether it holds.

Does her reply end the examination? It does if she says she has nothing more to ask, or leaves the rest to them without asking anything. Answering a question of theirs about the statement, a definition or the round doesn't end it, and neither does a goodbye to a student who's giving up. Ending isn't a claim, so it never makes Holds false.
